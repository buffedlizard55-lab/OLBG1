What today's numbers actually show, including the parts that do not flatter the desk.

1. The band rule and the probability rule point in opposite directions

Converting the brief's odds window to probabilities: −250 = 71.4%, +10000 = 0.99%. A 70% minimum therefore leaves a sliver of 1.4 percentage points. In practice a genuine 70%–71.4% favourite is a coin-flip-plus-a-nudge, exactly the price range where bookmakers hold their margin and where our own estimate is least distinguishable from the market. Today we found two selections above 70% and both are far above it, so both fall outside the cap.

This is worth a decision from the desk owner: either the band is a display preference (publish picks at any price and show the band), or the 70% rule is a preference (publish ≥70% and ignore the cap). Keeping both as hard filters produces an empty set most days.

2. Where our number and the market disagree

Tampa Bay @ Colorado. Our record-based estimate gives Tampa Bay 69.2%; the NHL's own published quotes de-vig to about 46.4% for the same team. The gap is 22.8 points. Two readings are on the table: the Lightning are 7-2-0 and the Avalanche 5-4-1, but Colorado have outscored opponents 41-31 while Tampa Bay's goal difference is only +8, and the market is reacting to underlying play rather than the win column. We publish the disagreement instead of picking a winner between the two.

3. Sample sizes are the real limit on confidence

URC form is built on two or three matches per club. Our margins are shrunk by n ÷ (n+4) precisely because two matches is not evidence of a season-long level; a club that has played two matches keeps only a third of its raw margin. MLB, by contrast, is built on 162 games, which is why the Brewers' 103-59 record moves their price and a 9-game NHL record barely does.
